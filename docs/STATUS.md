# Ibid add-in status

## Product direction
Ibid is an EU-law Word add-in for lawyers. It identifies references in footnotes and presents official source material from CJEU/CURIA, EUR-Lex, and European Commission registers inside the task pane. It assists source inspection; it does not assess legal correctness.

## Current implementation
- Individual Word footnotes are read and presented as review items. The pane follows the cursor: putting the caret on a citation, or on its reference mark in the body, shows that citation's source at the top of the pane. Word gives an add-in no way to draw beside the text — the document canvas is Word's own, and a dialog opens centred rather than next to what it explains — so following the cursor is what a popup at the citation would have been.
- What still needs a decision is said in one line beside the document, not listed. Which footnotes they are is a question the cursor answers; that anything is waiting at all is not, and a reviewer moving through a document would otherwise finish believing every citation resolved. The full footnote list remains where the cursor cannot be followed — the browser preview, or a Word build whose selection events do not register — because there it is the only route to a citation.
- Citations the drafter wrote into the running text are read too, not only the ones in notes. The parenthetical form is ordinary in prose — "the Court has held otherwise (Case C-293/12, para. 40)" — and to Ibid those did not exist at all: the body was read only to find notes a PDF conversion had flattened into it. Only what sits inside the parentheses is offered, and that bound is what keeps it honest: detection reaches outwards from a citation for its pinpoint, so turning it loose on running prose would let a sentence supply a paragraph number to a citation that never carried one. A parenthesis is a boundary the drafter drew, and what is inside it is treated exactly as a footnote's text is. Nested brackets are read to the outer close (`(Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 17(1))` is one citation, not a truncated one), a reference mark a conversion left behind (`(90)`, `(48)`) is not a citation, and a paragraph already read as a note is never read a second time as prose.
- EU-first recognition covers ECLI identifiers, CJEU/General Court case numbers, EU directives, regulations, recommendations, published EU decisions, DG Competition's own case numbers (`AT.`/`SA.`/`M.`), and Treaty articles (`Article 101 TFEU`, `Article 6(3) TEU`, `Article 47 of the Charter`, including the French `TFUE`/`TUE`/`Charte des droits fondamentaux` and `CFR`/`CDFUE` short forms). Legislation is recognised in both the pre-2015 citation style (`Directive 2002/58/EC`, `Regulation (EC) No 1049/2001`) and the current style (`Regulation (EU) 2016/679`, or informally without the bracket), including the number-order reversal between conventions. Article/point locators are recognised from common abbreviations and symbols too (`Art.`, `para.`/`paras.`, `§`, `¶`), not just the spelled-out words, and a paragraph within an article (`Art. 8(5)`) is distinguished from the article as a whole. A pinpoint list keeps every item cited "et seq." (also `ff.`, `et seqq.`, and the French `et s.`/`et suiv.`): "paragraphs 189 et seq., 1324 et seq. and 1398 et seq." used to stop at 189, and the pane showed one recital of Tata Steel/thyssenkrupp as all three. Each passage is shown on its own, the pane says that the paragraphs after one cited "et seq." are not shown, and a cited paragraph the text does not have is named rather than dropped while the others are presented as the passage cited.
- Short-form citations are resolved against the document, not looked up in isolation. A footnote reading only `Akzo Nobel, para. 40` carries no information on its own — it only means anything relative to a full citation stated earlier — so Ibid reads the whole document in order, registers each full citation under the short forms it could later be referred to by (any short form the drafter declared in quotes, plus the shortened case names a drafter plausibly uses), and resolves later references back to it. A footnote is split on `;` first, so one authority in a multi-authority footnote can never take the next one's pinpoint.
- A short form that matches more than one authority is never guessed at. It is reported as unresolved and no source is fetched for it — a wrong citation shown with full confidence is worse than a flagged gap. Only a `resolved` citation ever carries an identifier; everything else keeps its candidates separate, so nothing downstream can fetch on the strength of a guess.
- The reviewer resolves those cases in the pane. An ambiguous reference offers its candidates; a reference matching nothing offers every authority the document itself establishes; a name Ibid only recognises from its frequently-cited-cases list is offered as a suggestion to confirm rather than presented as established, since that is outside knowledge rather than something the document said. Confirming applies to that short form for the rest of the document, and is never carried into another document.
- Confirmation is asked for only where Ibid genuinely does not know. Prompting on every citation would make the prompt a reflex rather than a decision, and would end up laundering guesses as human sign-off. Instead every resolved citation states how it was resolved — from a short form the document defines, inferred from a case name cited in full earlier, or confirmed by the reviewer — so the reviewer can see what to check without being asked to click through what is not in doubt.
- Back-references — `Ibid.`, `Id.`, `Ibidem`, `supra note 4` — are resolved to the authority they point at, including through a chain of them. Where the text pointed at established exactly one authority these are not guesses to be confirmed but statements of what the word means, so they resolve outright and say where they read it: "Read as the authority cited immediately before it, in footnote 12." A bare `Ibid.` repeats the pinpoint as well as the authority; `Ibid., para. 44` keeps the authority and states a new one. Where the text pointed at cites several authorities, the reference is reported ambiguous with its candidates ordered nearest-cited first — the conventional reading offered as a hint, never applied. A back-reference never points at an authority the document did not itself establish, so it cannot inherit a suggestion or a guess, and confirming one applies to that single reference rather than to every `Ibid.` in the document, since each means whatever precedes it.
- Pinpoints are parsed as lists, not single numbers: `paras 40–44, 46 and 48` and `§§ 40-44, 46` report every paragraph cited, and every paragraph cited is retrieved: `paras 57–65` returns all nine, and `paras 62 and 65` returns both with the gap between them marked, rather than the span implied by their endpoints.
- A judgment or opinion covering joined cases is one authority, however the footnote writes the group — including the ordinary shape that gives each member its own parties ("Joined Cases C-293/12 Digital Rights Ireland and C-594/12 Seitlinger and Others"), which used to be read as two separate authorities. This matters beyond tidiness: CELLAR files a joined judgment under one of its case numbers and mints nothing at all for the others — `62012CJ0293` serves Digital Rights Ireland while `62012CJ0594` does not exist — so a member reported on its own reached a dead identifier and the reviewer got a link instead of the passage. Every number the group states is now carried, and the source is retrieved under whichever one CELLAR actually holds.
- Citations are normalised to CELEX where derivation is reliable, such as `C-293/12` → `62012CJ0293`, `Regulation (EU) 2016/679` → `32016R0679`, and `Article 101 TFEU` → `12016E101` (individual treaty articles carry their own dedicated CELEX, distinct from the whole-treaty document). Case numbers are recognised with the non-breaking hyphen CURIA's own documents use and with their procedural suffix (`C-550/07 P`, `T-286/09 RENV`).
- The local API retrieves a bounded official EUR-Lex/CELLAR passage and focuses on the cited point or Article, across older (2010s-and-earlier, `text/html`-only) and newer (`application/xhtml+xml`) legislation formats, and across four distinct judgment paragraph-numbering conventions spanning the 1990s to 2025 — all confirmed live against real documents, including a real client document that surfaced most of this. A citation like "Art. 8(5)" narrows all the way to just paragraph 5 of Article 8, not the whole article, across both legislation markup eras. It has configurable authentication headers, timeout, cache, retry/backoff, and request spacing. It also verifies a response is a genuine CELLAR document before showing it — anonymous access was observed live serving a bot-verification page with an ordinary `HTTP 200`, which would otherwise be decoded and shown as if it were the source text.
- Retrieval caches the **document**, not the excerpt, so an authority cited twenty times in a brief is downloaded once and each pinpoint is cut out of it locally. CELLAR serves no compression, so that download is the full 149KB of a judgment every time it is repeated — which it was, once per pinpoint, before this. The cache survives a restart and is confirmed against EUR-Lex on every use: a conditional request that CELLAR answers `304` with no body, measured live at ~270ms against ~1.5s for the document itself. There is deliberately no expiry — a published EU legal text does not change, so revalidation rather than a timer is what keeps it correct.
- Every retrieved passage says when it was last confirmed: "Verified against EUR-Lex at 14:32", with the date added once that is no longer today. Stated as a fact rather than as a disclaimer, because that is what it is — a revalidated cache is the official text with proof, not a copy with a caveat.
- Opening a document starts the retrieval rather than waiting for a click. The pane already knows every citation before anything is selected, so it deduplicates them to distinct authorities — `Ibid.` and `supra` chains resolved to what they point at — and works through them in reading order in the background, promoting whatever the cursor lands near and saying honestly how far it has got ("Retrieved 8 of 23 sources"). Everything stops when the document changes or the pane closes. The requests stay one at a time and the spacing between them is unchanged: the gain is starting earlier, not going faster, and a burst of parallel fetches is exactly what a public service's anti-bot protection is watching for.
- A citation that quotes an ECLI is retrieved by that ECLI first, and by the CELEX Ibid derives only where CELLAR does not know the ECLI. The CELEX is inferred — its sector letter from the document type, its year from the case number — while the ECLI is the name the issuing court gave the document the drafter wrote down, and the two can disagree: the 2026 draft merger guidelines cite "Judgment of 15 December 2002, Superleague v FIFA, C-333/21, EU:C:2022:993, paragraph 251", the word "Judgment" derived the judgment, and paragraph 251 of the judgment went on screen, while EU:C:2022:993 is Advocate General Rantos's Opinion. The retrieved document's own heading (`JUDGMENT OF THE COURT`, `OPINION OF ADVOCATE GENERAL …`, `ORDER OF THE …`, and the French) then titles it, and where it is not what the footnote called it the pane says so. CELLAR also turns out to hold some case law under the ECLI and not the CELEX. Every citation the corpus run had recorded as unavailable is retrieved this way: recent orders of the President of the General Court and of the Vice-President of the Court, and a 2005 judgment, all of which previously showed a link telling the reader to go and look the passage up themselves.
- A citation CELLAR genuinely does not hold, under either identifier, reaches its official CURIA link in a single request rather than four. CELLAR distinguishes "this identifier does not exist" from "this rendition of it does not", in the body of an otherwise identical `404`, and only the second is worth trying another format or language for. Measured live: 59ms, against roughly four seconds before.
- CJEU/General Court case citations get the same treatment: when a citation is confidently the main judgment, the API fetches the actual judgment text from EUR-Lex/CELLAR (which mirrors CJEU case law under its own CELEX) and focuses it on the cited point — the lawyer reads the real paragraph inline instead of following a search link. Advocate General opinions and orders are retrieved the same way: the CELEX sector is derived from the document type (`CC` for an opinion, `CO`/`TO` for an order), so the lawyer reads the opinion itself rather than a link to the case record. An opinion citing no case number takes it from the judgment the document cites elsewhere. Any fetch failure falls back to the official CURIA link, which stays the floor for documents CELLAR does not mirror. A Commission competition citation now reaches the **decision itself, and the cited recital inside it** — the first source family Ibid reads out of a PDF rather than out of CELLAR's HTML. Where the decision is is read from the case data the Commission publishes as open data on `data.europa.eu` under its own reuse notice: `AT.37990` resolves to the prohibition decision it was cited from, `AT.40178` to its Settlement Decision, `M.8124` to Microsoft/LinkedIn. The decision is then fetched once, its text extracted with `pdfjs-dist`, and the cited recital cut out of it locally — so "Case M.8124, paragraph 350" shows paragraph 350, and a second citation of the same decision costs a conditional request the Commission answers `304` with no body. Four refusals hold the accuracy line: the footnotes are dropped before the matcher sees them, by the size they are set in — a decision's page prints its recitals at 12pt and its footnotes at 10pt and 8pt, and without that the cited recital collected the footnote block printed under it (this also beats `pdftotext`, which counts two of Intel's footnote cross-references as recital anchors); a recital is then anchored only as `(350)` at the start of a line, never as a bare number, because the same decision carries a footnote 350; where a case was decided more than once, which decision was meant is settled by which one actually carries the cited recital rather than by its date — on Dow/DuPont (`M.7932`) the decision of 27 March 2017 carries 5,269 recitals including 1975 and neither of the two of 28 July 2017 carries any — and where two of them carry it the citation genuinely does not distinguish them, so both are offered and neither is opened; and a decision published as a scan is reported as one rather than shown as an empty passage. A case number is checked against the name written beside it (`Case M.7967 – Ball/Rexam`), using the case titles in the same open data, and where they disagree the name wins: the case whose register title is that name is shown, under a warning that says what the footnote wrote, what the register files that number as, and which case is shown instead — or, where no single case carries the name, that no decision is shown. The Commission's own 2026 draft merger guidelines carry seven such numbers, in nine of their 353 case citations (M.7967 for Ball/Rexam's M.7567, M.11506 for Parker/Meggitt's M.10506, M.9596 for EssilorLuxottica/GrandVision's M.9569, M.6461 for Outokumpu/Inoxum's M.6471, M.11771 for Pfizer/Seagen's M.11177, and M.9376 and M.9706, never used, for Siemens/Alstom's M.8677 and Novelis/Aleris's M.9076); all nine are corrected and the other 344 are left as cited. A name that shares one word with the title agrees, so shortened and misspelt names (`BSCH/Champalimaud`, `Boeing/Sprit`) are not flagged. The card is titled as the register titles the case (`M.8713 – TATA STEEL / THYSSENKRUPP / JV`), and the warning is said once for the citation, above however many of the case's decisions are offered. A decision cited by a numbered section (`section 9.1.3.3.7`, `Sections 7.5-7.7`, `sections 8.3.4.1 and 8.4.4.1`) shows that section: from its heading to the next heading that is neither inside it nor before it in the numbering, skipping the table of contents' entries by their dot leaders, cut at a line and marked `[…]` past the excerpt cap. On Norsk Hydro/Alumetal, section 9.1.3.3.7 is recitals (299) to (321). An annex's section or paragraph (`Annex 5, Section 6`, `Annex A, paragraph 8`, `Economic Annex, paragraph 45`) is not anchored, since the decision's own section 6 or recital 8 is another passage, and neither is a lettered paragraph (`paragraph 1605a`), which is not the paragraph its number names. A case's pinpoint is read only from its own part of the footnote — up to the next authority cited and the end of its sentence, with the parenthesis describing it (`(Article 21(3) decision of 20.07.1999)`) passed over — because each of those let a decision take a passage the drafter cited for something else. A decision already read is shown at once rather than after it is confirmed: confirming first cost the reviewer a turn at the one-request-per-second wire for every decision in the case, so the pane shows the held passage dated when it was last confirmed ("Verified against the Commission on … ; checking for changes") and confirms it once warming has drained, replacing the passage and saying so if the Commission has published a different version since. On by default, because every way it can fail ends at the case-register link — the pane a reviewer had before this existed — so the worst outcome of leaving it on is the behaviour of leaving it off; `IBID_COMMISSION_CASE_DATA=off` turns it off for a deployment whose IT wants to approve the outbound hosts first.
- Source text is retrieved in English where the document was published in English, and in French where it was not — CELLAR answers 404 for a language a document never had, so this is a real fallback rather than a preference. (Re-confirmed live on 21 August 2026: `Accept-Language: gle` 404s for both a directive and a judgment. A judgment returning Maltese for `mlt` is not a counter-example — CJEU judgments are translated into every official language except Irish, so Maltese genuinely exists for it.) A French passage is labelled as such — and the label is read off the retrieved document rather than assumed from what was asked for, because a French passage recorded as English would be shown unlabelled, untranslated, and as though the Court had written it that way. Where a translator is configured it is shown in English instead, marked as a translation and carrying a link to the authentic French, because a translation is not the authority; the Court's own English is always preferred over a machine's, and a translation failure falls back to the published French rather than to nothing. No translator is wired today, by decision: the corpus needing one was measured live at **207 documents** (5.9M characters, ~$163 to translate), and the question of paying for it is open. Until then a French-only passage is shown in French and labelled.
- The Word pane displays loading, success, empty, unconfirmed, and retrieval-error states, and a citation already retrieved in the background opens with no loading state at all. A request that never reached Ibid's server says so ("Ibid could not reach its server") rather than that the source could not be retrieved, which is a claim about the authority. An answer is shown only under the citation it was asked for: a Commission decision read cold takes minutes (207s and 224s measured on the draft merger guidelines), and the answer to one footnote used to replace whatever the reviewer had moved on to, failure included. It is held when it arrives, so going back opens it at once, and going back before it arrives waits for the same request rather than sending another. A confirmed citation always exposes the official source link; an unconfirmed one shows its candidate authorities instead and does not offer a source, since Ibid does not know which document it would be showing.

## Development
Run `npm run dev` from the repository root. This builds and starts the local resolver and Vite task-pane server. If port 4000 is occupied, use `IBID_API_PORT=4001 npm run dev` so both the API and Vite proxy use the same alternate port.

Run `npm run verify` to lint, type-check the tests, run the 721 tests, and build. `addin/test/App.test.tsx` still hangs intermittently between tests (see HANDOFF.md); re-run when a verify stalls there.

`npm run answer-key` compares what the pane reports for a real document with an answer key of every citation in it, written from the published PDF without reference to Ibid's output — the 2026 draft merger guidelines so far, 478 footnotes and 624 citations. It fails on any citation shown at the wrong document or the wrong place, on any gap not already listed with a reason, and on any listed gap that has since been fixed. It sits outside `verify` because it needs the document in `samples/`, which is not in the repository.

Two more checks sit outside it because they need something else a unit test does not have. `npm run
corpus` runs detection over real Commission decisions and Advocate General opinions and
grades every identifier it derives against the ECLI CELLAR declares for that document — it
needs the network and takes minutes. `npm run word-check` opens the sample documents in real
Word and reports every footnote where the corpus reader and Word disagree — it needs Word on
Windows. Both exit non-zero on a finding. `scripts/README.md` explains what each measures,
and what a zero in each column does not mean.

The API server keeps the EU documents it retrieves in a cache directory outside the repository (`~/.cache/ibid/documents` by default) and prints the path it is using at startup. `IBID_CACHE_DIR` moves it; `IBID_CACHE_ENTRIES=0` switches it off. It holds public EU legal text only — nothing from the user's document ever reaches the server. See [DATA-FLOW.md](DATA-FLOW.md).

For the detailed implementation handoff and test checklist, see [HANDOFF.md](HANDOFF.md).

## Production hardening
- Set `IBID_USER_AGENT` to a descriptive agent carrying a real contact address, and configure the other server-side variables described in `api/README.md`. There is no authorised tier to provision: CELLAR's REST interface is public, and the registrable EUR-Lex web service is a SOAP *search* API that does not retrieve documents. What protects access is being an identifiable, well-behaved client — the resolver already spaces, backs off, and caches; identification was the missing piece. Anonymous access was once observed being served a bot-verification page under normal request volume, which is the failure this addresses.
- `api/` now has one runtime dependency, `pdfjs-dist`, where it had none — and "none" was a claim the security review leaned on. It is one Apache-2.0 package with no transitive dependencies of its own; its only declared dependency, `@napi-rs/canvas`, is optional, exists for rendering pages to images, is never reached by text extraction, and is left out by installing with `--omit=optional`. A dependency-free extractor was written first and refused: it recovers 85–95% of the characters and loses the line structure the recital anchors depend on, finding 12 recital markers where a decision has 1,244. Decide whether the server deployment pins `--omit=optional`, since a plain `npm install` pulls the 34MB native binary and a blanket `.npmrc` would also drop Rollup's platform binary, which the pane's build needs.
- A case publishing more than four decisions keeps the link rather than reading them, since each candidate is a download. Raising the bound is cheap but costs a first lookup more; the cache makes every later citation of the same case free either way.
- Extend the Commission case data to State aid. Antitrust (2.9MB) and mergers (39MB) are indexed; the State aid distribution is 698MB and needs a streaming parse and a disk index rather than a map built in memory.
